Anterior Cervical Discectomy and Fusion

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) addresses cervical nerve-root or spinal cord compression through an anterior approach to the disc space. Following discectomy and decompression, an interbody graft or cage reconstructs the space and supports segmental stability while fusion develops. [1]

Clinical presentation and indications

Cervical radiculopathy reflects dysfunction of a nerve root. A patient may describe pain radiating into an arm, numbness or weakness in a pattern that helps localize the affected root. Cervical myelopathy reflects spinal cord dysfunction: difficulty with hand dexterity, gait imbalance and hyperreflexia are examples of findings that can point to cord dysfunction. The two syndromes can coexist, and distinguishing root from cord findings helps localize the clinically relevant compression.

Imaging must be interpreted in the context of the clinical examination: a degenerative disc abnormality alone does not establish the source of symptoms. The side, level and pattern of compression should correlate with the neurologic findings. Severity, progression and response to appropriate nonsurgical care all enter the decision; an individual with progressive neurologic dysfunction may need a different assessment from someone with stable symptoms. [1]

Anatomy and rationale for the anterior approach

The disc lies anterior to the spinal canal; the anterior approach therefore provides direct access to selected ventral disc and osteophyte pathology through the disc space. In the neck, the working corridor lies between the carotid sheath laterally and the trachea and esophagus medially. The longus colli muscles lie on the anterior cervical spine; the sympathetic chain is farther lateral. These relationships explain why exposure requires care even before the spine is reached.

Once the disc is removed and the neural structures are decompressed, an interbody graft or cage occupies the space. A plate or integrated fixation device may provide additional stability. This reconstruction holds the segment while bone healing develops; a visible cage or plate on an immediate image does not mean that fusion is already complete. The best approach and construct depend on the pattern of disease, alignment and other clinical factors. [1]

Preoperative evaluation and planning

Preoperative evaluation begins with localization of the neurologic deficit and correlation with the suspected level. MRI helps demonstrate the cord, nerve roots, discs and other soft tissues. Radiographs help assess alignment and the relevant levels, and CT can help characterize bony compression when appropriate. A surface skin crease does not reliably identify a disc space: intraoperative localization is a separate confirmation step.

The assessment also identifies factors that can affect exposure and healing. Previous anterior neck surgery, baseline voice or swallowing difficulty and airway limitations matter because the approach passes close to the aerodigestive tract. Bone quality, smoking, medications, comorbidities and the number of levels influence the broader risk and reconstruction discussion. The choice of an anterior or alternative route depends on the distribution of compression, alignment and the clinical objective.

Outcomes and complications

Decompression aims to relieve pressure, but improvement is not always immediate or complete. Nerve and spinal cord recovery depend on the preoperative condition and many other factors. Follow-up connects the patient's symptoms and neurologic examination to imaging of alignment, implant position and, over time, fusion. Outcomes reported in different studies are not interchangeable because diagnoses, levels and follow-up vary. [2]

Postoperative dysphagia and voice changes reflect the vulnerability of structures adjacent to the anterior neck corridor; an expanding hematoma can threaten the airway. Other potential complications include injury to the esophagus, vessels, nerves or spinal cord, infection, implant problems and failure of fusion. These vary in likelihood and timing. Anatomical relationships in the simulation

The guide follows the exposure from skin through platysma and the deeper tissue planes to the anterior vertebral bodies. The relationship of the visceral structures medially and carotid sheath laterally defines the corridor; the selected disc space is identified between its adjacent vertebral bodies.

At decompression, the posterior boundary of the disc space separates the operative field from the neural structures beyond it. Reconstruction provides immediate structural support, whereas fusion is a subsequent biological process. The guide offers more than one configuration to illustrate how the extent and construct can change; it does not establish that one choice is best for a particular patient.
